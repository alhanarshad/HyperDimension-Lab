You cannot fit a higher-dimensional object onto a screen without losing information. But you can explore its shadows, turn it in unfamiliar planes, and follow its cross-sections.

01 / COORDINATES

One number for every direction

A point in 3D needs three coordinates: x, y and z. In 4D, add w. In 5D and 6D, add v and u. Each coordinate describes an independent perpendicular direction in Euclidean space.

These are spatial mathematical dimensions. The fourth coordinate here is not time; animation simply changes the orientation of the object.

3D → (x, y, z) 4D → (x, y, z, w) 5D → (x, y, z, w, v) 6D → (x, y, z, w, v, u)
02 / TWO WAYS TO LOOK

A shadow or a section?

Projection maps the whole object into fewer dimensions. Distinct vertices may overlap; edges may cross on the screen even when they never meet in the original object.

Slice intersects the object with a hyperplane at a chosen value of its last coordinate. Only the intersection remains. Moving the plane reveals how the object changes across that extra direction.

A 4D slice is 3D. A 5D slice is 4D; a 6D slice is 5D. Those larger slices still need projection to become visible.

03 / MOTION

Rotate in a plane, not around an extra axis

Choose two coordinates i and j. A rotation mixes just those coordinates while leaving the others unchanged. Every pair provides a coordinate rotation plane: 6 in 4D, 10 in 5D and 15 in 6D. The general count is n(n − 1)/2.

xᵢ′ = cos(θ) xᵢ − sin(θ) xⱼ xⱼ′ = sin(θ) xᵢ + cos(θ) xⱼ

These plane rotations preserve lengths, distances and angles before projection. If a projected edge appears to stretch, that is an effect of the view. Combining rotations in different planes generally depends on their order; rotations in disjoint coordinate planes commute.

Dragging the scene changes the familiar 3D viewing angle. The coordinate-plane controls rotate the original higher-dimensional object. Try both to feel the difference.

04 / THE VIEW

How projection works

Orthographic: discard the extra coordinate at each stage. Parallel lines remain parallel, but hidden directions collapse.

Perspective: when reducing from k dimensions to k − 1, multiply the retained coordinates by d/(d − xₖ), where d is the projection distance. Repeat until three coordinates remain, then draw that view on the screen.

The repeated perspective construction is one useful visualization convention. It is not a unique or complete picture of the original shape.

05 / THE SECTION

Where a slice comes from

For an edge from a to b, a slice at last-coordinate value s crosses at parameter t = (s − aₖ)/(bₖ − aₖ). If t is between 0 and 1, the intersection point is a + t(b − a).

Intersecting each polygonal face connects these points into the slice's wireframe. Coplanar and boundary cases require handling whole edges and merging coincident intersections.

The explorer shows vertices and edges, not filled solids. A slice outside the object's coordinate range is empty. Numerical calculations use floating-point precision.

06 / THE COLLECTION

Six families, three dimensions

FamilyWhat to notice
HypercubeAll combinations of two coordinate values. An n-cube has 2ⁿ vertices and n · 2ⁿ⁻¹ edges. The 4D example is the tesseract.
SimplexThe higher-dimensional triangle or tetrahedron: n + 1 equidistant vertices, with every pair connected.
Cross-polytopeThe higher-dimensional octahedron: two vertices on each coordinate axis. Each vertex connects to every vertex except itself and its opposite.
HyperrectangleA stretched hypercube. Its unequal side lengths make it easier to track directions as the object turns.
Duoprism familyIn 4D, the Cartesian product of two regular hexagons. The 5D and 6D examples add interval factors, making prisms over this 4D object.
24-cell familyThe 4D regular 24-cell has 24 vertices and 96 edges. In 5D and 6D, the examples are prisms over it, not new regular 24-cells.

Objects are scaled for viewing. Counts describe mathematical connectivity; overlapping projected points can make the visible count appear smaller.

07 / TRY IT YOURSELF

Four small experiments

Experiment 01 · 4D

Meet the tesseract

Choose the 4D hypercube. Pause the animation and reset it. Compare orthographic and perspective views, then change the x–w rotation. Follow one connected group of edges as it moves through the projection.

Experiment 02 · 4D

Pass a world through a world

Use an unrotated 4D hypercube and switch to slice mode. Move the slice from one side to the other: the interior sections are cubes. Rotate in x–w, then repeat. The section now changes as the slicing plane moves.

Experiment 03 · 5D

Find the new rotation planes

Choose a 5D simplex. It has six vertices. Use a plane involving the fifth coordinate, then compare a familiar x–y rotation. The object stays rigid in 5D even as its projection appears to deform.

Experiment 04 · 6D

More structure, one screen

Choose the 6D hypercube: 64 vertices and 192 edges. Switch between projection styles and turn one coordinate plane at a time. Try the cross-polytope next: its 12 vertices provide a less crowded comparison.
